Bleeding after menopause

4 min read Clinically reviewed · September 28, 2026 Updated: October 27, 2026 MenopausePostmenopause

Summary

This article has a single message: any bleeding 12 months or more after your last period, even a single episode of spotting, should be assessed by a doctor. In the great majority of cases the cause is benign: thinning of the vagina or the lining of the womb, a polyp, bleeding related to HRT. But in roughly one woman in ten the cause is cancer of the womb — and that cancer is highly treatable when caught at an early stage. Bleeding is usually the first and only sign. The assessment is simple, quick and usually painless: an ultrasound and, if needed, a sample from the lining of the womb.

What is happening?

After menopause the lining of the womb thins and does not normally bleed. Bleeding happens for these reasons:

  • Vaginal or endometrial atrophy (thinning): The commonest cause. Thinned tissue is easily irritated and bleeds; it can be treated (see the separate article on GSM).
  • Polyps: Benign growths on the lining of the womb or the cervix; usually removed with a simple procedure.
  • Bleeding related to HRT: In a cyclical regimen the expected withdrawal bleed is normal. In a continuous combined regimen spotting can occur in the first 6 months; bleeding that continues beyond 6 months, or starts later, is investigated.
  • Endometrial hyperplasia: Excessive thickening of the lining; some types are precancerous and are treated.
  • Endometrial (womb) cancer: Found in about 9% of cases of postmenopausal bleeding. Obesity, diabetes, never having given birth, late menopause, unopposed estrogen use and tamoxifen increase the risk.
  • Other causes: Cervical lesions, bleeding from the urinary tract or bowel mistaken for vaginal bleeding, and certain medicines (blood thinners).

What do we know?

  • The cancer rate is not negligible: In a meta-analysis covering 129 studies, endometrial cancer was found in about 9% of women with postmenopausal bleeding; conversely, in more than 90% of women with endometrial cancer the first symptom was postmenopausal bleeding 2. In other words, bleeding is almost always an opportunity to catch the disease early.
  • The first step is a transvaginal ultrasound: ACOG accepts measuring the thickness of the lining of the womb by ultrasound as the first assessment; if the thickness is 4 mm or less the likelihood of cancer is very low, and in a woman not taking HRT with a single episode of bleeding a biopsy may not be needed. If the thickness is above 4 mm, if it cannot be measured, or if the bleeding recurs, a sample is taken from the lining 1.
  • The biopsy is usually done in the office: Taking a sample from the lining of the womb with a fine cannula is a procedure that takes a few minutes and usually needs no anesthetic; where it is inconclusive, a hysteroscopy (viewing the inside of the womb with a camera) is planned 1.
  • Referral is fast: In the UK, postmenopausal bleeding over the age of 55 is a symptom requiring specialist assessment within two weeks for suspected cancer; the same pathway is considered under 55 3.
  • Investigation is needed in HRT users too: Guidelines recommend assessing bleeding that falls outside the expected pattern, is prolonged or recurs, even where it is thought to be HRT-related; HRT should be recorded not as an "explanation" for the bleeding but as information your doctor will take into account after it 4.

What does this give you?

  • Don't wait, but don't panic either: In nine women out of ten the cause is benign. For the tenth, coming forward early saves lives. In both cases the thing to do is the same: make an appointment.
  • Record it: The date of the bleeding, the amount (spotting or like a period), how long it lasted, whether it happened after sex, and your regimen if you are taking HRT. You can flag bleeding after menopause in Lumia; it records this, it does not interpret it.
  • Try to identify where it came from: If you are not sure whether the blood came from your urine, your stool or your vagina, tell your doctor; it changes the direction of the assessment.
  • Even if it is "just dryness": Bleeding due to atrophy is a treatable problem; once it is diagnosed, vaginal estrogen and moisturizers prevent these recurrences.
  • Know the risk factors: If you have obesity, diabetes, are taking tamoxifen, or have a family history of womb or bowel cancer, your doctor will plan the assessment accordingly.

When to see a doctor?

  • Any bleeding or spotting after 12 months without a period; without exception, and soon.
  • While using HRT, bleeding outside the expected pattern, or bleeding that continues beyond 6 months or restarts on a continuous regimen.
  • Bleeding together with pelvic pain, weight loss, abdominal swelling or loss of appetite.
  • Heavy bleeding (like a period or more); it should be assessed the same day.

These signs should be assessed without delay.

What you can ask your doctor

  • Based on my ultrasound result, do I need a sample taken from the lining of my womb?
  • If I am using HRT, could this bleeding be due to my regimen; does it still need investigating?
  • If the bleeding is due to vaginal thinning, which treatments are suitable?
