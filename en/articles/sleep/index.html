Why does sleep break down?

5 min read Clinically reviewed · September 28, 2026 Updated: October 27, 2026 PerimenopauseMenopausePostmenopause

Summary

About half of women in perimenopause and early postmenopause report a sleep problem: not being able to fall asleep, waking in the night and not getting back to sleep, waking too early in the morning. The causes stack on top of each other: night sweats, mood changes, falling progesterone, sleep apnea becoming more common with age, and the general load of midlife. The most effective and lasting treatment is not a medicine but structured cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I). If night sweats are playing the leading role, targeting them also puts sleep right. Which route is the right one is something you decide together with your doctor.

What is happening?

Sleep breaks down through several channels in this period, and usually more than one is at work at once:

  • Night sweats: A flush wakes you; the real problem is not the sweating but not being able to fall back asleep afterwards.
  • Falling progesterone: Progesterone has a mildly calming effect on the brain; as ovulation becomes less frequent, that effect fades.
  • Mood and anxiety: Anxiety makes falling asleep harder, and depressive symptoms increase early-morning waking. Disturbed sleep then feeds both back.
  • Sleep apnea: After menopause, the tone of the upper airway muscles and the distribution of body fat change, and the frequency of apnea approaches that in men. In women it often goes unnoticed, because it can present as fatigue, morning headaches and insomnia without the classic snoring.
  • Restless legs and getting up to urinate: Both can increase in this period.
  • Habits around sleep: Lying awake in bed for long stretches, napping during the day, screens and caffeine make insomnia "learned"; it can persist even once hormones have settled.

What do we know?

  • The frequency rises markedly: In SWAN, sleep disturbance was significantly higher in late perimenopause and early postmenopause than in the reproductive years; vasomotor symptoms are one of the strongest predictors, but they do not explain it on their own 1 2.
  • CBT-I is first-line: The American Academy of Sleep Medicine places CBT-I ahead of medication for chronic insomnia, with a strong recommendation 3. In the MsFLASH trial in menopausal women, telephone-delivered CBT-I significantly reduced insomnia severity and the effect lasted 6 months 4. CBT-I is a 4–8 session program made up of sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring and sleep hygiene.
  • HRT is effective for insomnia driven by night sweats: If vasomotor symptoms are breaking up your sleep, HRT improves sleep quality along with the symptom; it is not, however, a first-line treatment for insomnia without symptoms 5. Micronized progesterone taken at night has been observed to make falling asleep easier — a point your doctor may take into account when choosing a regimen.
  • Non-hormonal medicines: Gabapentin has shown benefit for night sweats and sleep, and some NK3 antagonists have shown a positive result for sleep as well 6. Long-term use of sleeping tablets is limited in the guidelines because of dependence and the risk of falls.
  • Melatonin and supplements: The evidence for melatonin in menopausal insomnia is weak, and there is not enough evidence for herbal products 6.

What does this give you?

  • Separate out the cause first: If you wake up sweating, the target is the vasomotor symptom. If you wake without sweating and lie awake for hours, the target is the insomnia itself. If both are happening, there are two targets. Marking sleep and night sweats separately in Lumia makes that distinction visible.
  • The core principles of CBT-I can be applied today: Go to bed only when sleepy, get up if you haven't fallen asleep within 20 minutes, get up at the same time every day, don't nap, use the bed only for sleep. The sleep tool in Lumia takes you through these principles step by step; it does not replace clinical CBT-I.
  • The bedroom: Cool, dark, quiet. Breathable sheets and layered bedding make a practical difference for night sweats.
  • Caffeine and alcohol: Alcohol makes falling asleep easier but increases waking in the night and sweating. Cutting caffeine after midday makes a measurable difference for most people.
  • Fatigue can have causes other than a sleep problem: Thyroid problems, anemia, vitamin D deficiency and sleep apnea all need a doctor's assessment.

When to see a doctor?

  • Loud snoring, pauses in breathing during sleep (observed by a partner), morning headaches, excessive daytime sleepiness — a sleep apnea assessment is needed.
  • Insomnia together with marked low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of self-harm — get help straight away.
  • An irresistible urge to move the legs at night — restless legs syndrome and iron deficiency should be assessed.
  • If you are taking a sleeping tablet and can't sleep without it — a planned reduction with your doctor is needed.

These signs should be assessed without delay.
