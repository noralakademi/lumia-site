Brain fog and memory: temporary or permanent?

5 min read Clinically reviewed · September 28, 2026 Updated: October 27, 2026 PerimenopauseMenopausePostmenopause

Summary

Not being able to find a word, forgetting why you walked into a room, reading the same thing twice. More than half of women in perimenopause report difficulties like these, and for many it is here that the fear "is this the start of dementia?" arrives for the first time. The evidence is clear: during the menopausal transition there is a measurable, small and temporary decline in learning and verbal memory, and in most women it recovers in postmenopause. It is not a forerunner of dementia. When sleep, hot flashes and mood are put right, the fog largely lifts too.

What is happening?

Estrogen supports energy metabolism and the connections between nerve cells in the brain regions involved in memory and learning (the hippocampus, the prefrontal cortex). The fluctuation of estrogen in perimenopause makes those systems work temporarily less efficiently; the brain adapts to the new conditions, and performance usually returns to its earlier level in postmenopause.

But what actually carries brain fog is often indirect:

  • Broken sleep: Sleep is when memory is consolidated; sleep broken by night sweats directly lowers attention and recall the next day.
  • Hot flashes: Objectively measured frequent hot flashes have been associated with verbal memory performance.
  • Mood and anxiety: These consume attentional resources; "forgetfulness" is often really "failure to encode".
  • Load: Work, family and caring responsibilities peaking in the same years divide cognitive capacity.

What do we know?

  • The decline is real, small and temporary: In SWAN, women in late perimenopause failed to show the expected "practice effect" (improvement on repeat testing) on verbal learning and processing speed tests; this decline disappeared in postmenopause. The effect size, while noticeable in daily life, is not at the level of clinical impairment 1.
  • Brain fog is not dementia: Menopausal brain fog typically affects word-finding, attention and working memory; orientation, judgment and everyday functioning are preserved. Rapid progression or loss of function calls for a different assessment 2.
  • HRT is not given to protect cognition: Combined HRT started over the age of 65 did not reduce dementia risk in the WHIMS trial — it increased it 3. In recently postmenopausal women (KEEPS-Cog), 4 years of HRT neither improved nor worsened cognition 4. Guidelines do not recommend HRT for cognitive function; they note that HRT given for vasomotor symptoms may bring indirect benefit by improving sleep and symptoms 5.
  • Correctable causes come first: Sleep, depressive symptoms, thyroid problems, B12 deficiency and some medicines (antihistamines, sleeping tablets, certain painkillers) are common and treatable causes of cognitive complaints 2.

What does this give you?

  • Putting the fear in its place: Having brain fog in midlife does not mean your risk of dementia has risen. The symptom is temporary, and it is most often the shadow of another symptom (sleep, sweats, mood).
  • Fix sleep first: The single highest-yield step for a cognitive complaint is repairing sleep (see the separate article on sleep).
  • Use external memory: Notes, lists, calendars and reminders do not "make the brain lazy"; they reduce the load and prevent mistakes. Lumia's reminders do exactly this for medicines and appointments.
  • Evidence-based habits that protect brain health: Regular aerobic exercise, blood pressure control, not smoking, social connection, having hearing loss treated. None of these is specific to menopause, but this is the best time to start them.
  • Record it: Marking the "brain fog" symptom in Lumia alongside sleep and hot flashes shows you what the relationship looks like for you.

When to see a doctor?

  • Rapidly worsening forgetfulness; getting lost in familiar places; not recognizing familiar people.
  • Changes in personality, impaired judgment, being unable to manage everyday tasks such as paying bills or taking medicines.
  • Sudden difficulty speaking, asymmetry of the face, weakness in an arm — signs of a stroke, an emergency.
  • People close to you being more worried than you are: in dementia the person often does not notice it themselves, but those around them do.

These signs should be assessed without delay.

What you can ask your doctor

  • Is this forgetfulness consistent with the menopausal transition, or should it be assessed separately?
  • Have correctable causes such as sleep, mood and thyroid been checked?
  • Should I have a cognitive screening test?
