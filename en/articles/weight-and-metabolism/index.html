Does menopause make you put on weight?

5 min read Clinically reviewed · September 28, 2026 Updated: October 27, 2026 PerimenopauseMenopausePostmenopause

Summary

"I eat the same, I move the same, I'm putting on weight, and it's all going to my middle." There are two separate truths behind that sentence. The first: the weight itself rises with age more than with menopause; men show a similar trend in midlife. The second: menopause changes where the weight goes. As estrogen falls, fat shifts from the hips and thighs into the abdomen (visceral fat), and muscle mass declines. Even if your weight stays the same, your waist grows and your metabolic risk rises. That is why the target in this period is not "the number on the scale" but muscle and waist measurement.

What is happening?

  • The effect of age: In midlife, basal metabolism slows, daily movement decreases and muscle mass falls. This is independent of sex and amounts to roughly half a kilo a year.
  • The effect of menopause: As estrogen falls, fat distribution changes: a shift from fat under the skin to fat inside the abdomen. Abdominal fat behaves differently from subcutaneous fat; it worsens insulin resistance, blood lipids and inflammatory markers.
  • Muscle loss: Muscle is tissue that burns energy even at rest; losing it both slows metabolism and reduces strength.
  • Sleep and mood: Broken sleep disrupts appetite hormones (leptin, ghrelin); fatigue reduces movement; low mood affects eating behavior.

What do we know?

  • Weight goes with age, distribution with menopause: In SWAN, fat mass increased markedly across the menopausal transition while lean mass fell; this change started about two years before the final period and slowed two years after it. Total weight change, by contrast, was largely explained by age 1.
  • Abdominal fat determines metabolic risk: The American Heart Association describes the menopausal transition as a period in which cardiovascular risk rises markedly in women; increasing visceral fat, adverse changes in blood lipids and insulin resistance are what carry it 3. The International Menopause Society likewise uses the phrase "menopause changes fat distribution and risk" rather than "menopause makes you put on weight" 2.
  • HRT is not a weight-loss treatment, but it affects distribution: In randomized trials, HRT reduced the menopause-related accumulation of abdominal fat and insulin resistance; it has no marked effect on total weight. Guidelines do not recommend HRT for weight control; this is a side finding observed when it is given for vasomotor symptoms 4.
  • Losing weight reduces hot flashes: In women carrying excess weight, weight loss has the best evidence of any lifestyle intervention for reducing vasomotor symptoms 5.
  • Resistance exercise and protein: The two essential components of preserving muscle mass are progressive resistance exercise and adequate protein intake. Aerobic exercise alone does not prevent muscle loss 2.

What does this give you?

  • Watch the right indicator: Your waist measurement and how you feel mean more than the scale. Rapid, strict diets accelerate muscle loss — the least desirable outcome in this period.
  • Protect your muscle: Resistance exercise at least twice a week and a source of protein at every meal are the backbone of eating and movement in this period. Walking and cardio are valuable but do not take the place of muscle work.
  • Managing weight is hard until sleep is put right: see the article on sleep.
  • Metabolic monitoring: Waist measurement, blood pressure, fasting glucose or HbA1c and blood lipids should be monitored regularly in this period; your doctor decides how often.
  • Medicines: You can discuss medicinal options for weight management with your doctor.

When to see a doctor?

  • Rapid and unexplained weight gain or loss (a marked change within a few months).
  • Weight gain together with excessive thirst, frequent urination, blurred vision — blood sugar should be assessed.
  • Rapidly increasing swelling of the abdomen, feeling full early, pelvic pain — a gynecological assessment is needed.
  • If you feel your eating is out of your control, talk to your doctor about getting support.

These signs should be assessed without delay.

What you can ask your doctor

  • How should my waist measurement, blood sugar and cholesterol be monitored in this period?
  • Have my thyroid levels been checked?
  • Could I be referred to a dietitian or physiotherapist for an eating and exercise plan that suits me?
